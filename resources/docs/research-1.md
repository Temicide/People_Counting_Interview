Advanced Computer Vision Methodologies for Cross-Line Pedestrian Counting and Video Individual Counting: A State-of-the-Art SurveyTaxonomy and Theoretical Evolution of Vision-Based Pedestrian AnalyticsComputer vision-based pedestrian counting has evolved from raw frame-differencing algorithms to highly sophisticated, deeply integrated deep learning paradigms. To design or deploy an effective pedestrian counting system, it is necessary to classify these methodologies into distinct paradigms based on their spatial constraints, objective functions, and handling of temporal variables. The primary division in literature lies between Region of Interest (ROI) and Line of Interest (LOI) counting. While ROI methods estimate the instantaneous number of people within a predefined static area, LOI (commonly referred to as cross-line or line-crossing) methods focus on tracking objects over time to produce an instantaneous and cumulative flow rate.A major drawback of conventional image-level and ROI counting methods is their inability to capture the continuous passage of pedestrians over a temporal window. If frame-level density maps are simply integrated and summed across a video sequence, the same static or slow-moving individuals are inevitably counted multiple times, leading to a massive overestimation of pedestrian volume. Conversely, traditional LOI techniques, while effective for narrow doors and corridors, are structurally constrained to manual virtual lines. If a pedestrian enters or exits the camera's field of view without crossing the designated line, or if they stay entirely within the scene, they are completely missed.To reconcile these limitations, researchers have introduced the paradigm of Video Individual Counting (VIC). Unlike Single Image Crowd Counting (SICC) and Video Crowd Counting (VCC), which estimate frame-level counts, VIC tracks and counts unique pedestrians across video frames, ensuring that each individual is registered exactly once regardless of their trajectory or proximity to a virtual line.Historically, early pedestrian counting relied on handcrafted visual features. Techniques such as Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT), Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi (KLT) tracking, optical flow, frame-differencing, and background subtraction were used to capture motion trajectories. These motion-based techniques were computationally lightweight but suffered from severe sensitivity to illumination changes, camera ego-motion, and occlusions. Physics-inspired models subsequently attempted to model pedestrian groups using fluid dynamics, interaction forces, and complex crowd motion vector systems, treating dense pedestrian paths as macroscopic vector flows.The advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) shifted the field toward learning-based regression. Early deep architectures utilized random forest regressors to map local spatial features to density maps. This progressed to Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) and dilated convolutional networks like CSRNet, which expand the neural receptive field without losing spatial resolution via pooling operations. In this approach, an object detector is run frame-by-frame to generate localized bounding boxes, which are subsequently fed into a data association tracking algorithm. Geometric reasoning is then applied to determine when a tracked trajectory intersects a virtual counting line.Detector Architecture and Model Scaling EffectsThe accuracy and latency of a cross-line counting system are fundamentally bounded by the detector's capability. Modern real-time implementations leverage the YOLO (You Only Look Once) model family, specifically YOLOv5s, YOLOv8n, YOLO10, YOLO11, and the attention-centric YOLOv12, due to their highly optimized speed-accuracy trade-offs.Experimental evaluations on pedestrian safety indicate that model scale, or "brain size," is a critical factor in detection quality. While smaller models (such as YOLOv8n or YOLOv5s) execute significantly faster—rendering them ideal for resource-constrained edge devices—larger model sizes yield much higher average detection confidence and lower false-miss rates.Furthermore, the physical positioning of the camera relative to the pedestrian flow severely impacts detector confidence. Objects oriented perpendicularly and located closer to the camera lens are detected with the highest confidence. Conversely, pedestrians located at skewed angles or farther distances suffer from high-frequency bounding box drops and low confidence values.To mitigate these issues in static outdoor environments, the detector can be fine-tuned using localized, site-specific annotated images. Retraining with in-situ data helps the network adapt to the specific geometry, lighting conditions, and perspective distortions of the target camera stream.Cascaded Classification and Multi-Cue ClusteringIn complex multi-modal traffic environments, standard single-class detectors are often insufficient. Researchers have deployed cascaded detection systems to handle overlapping classes with similar visual appearances, such as pedestrians and bicyclists.A representative prototype system utilizes an stationary monocular uncalibrated camera to collect video streams. The first stage of the cascade utilizes a general object detector to extract regions of interest containing moving targets, without distinguishing their class. To minimize false alarms and optimize bounding box fit, the outputs of the detector are processed by a multi-cue clustering algorithm. This algorithm fuses three distinct inputs:Foreground detection masks from background subtraction.Geometric prior information of the scene.Temporal moving direction computed via dense optical flow.The clustered outputs are then fed into the second stage of the cascade, which is a dedicated classifier (e.g., using an Inception-based backbone) trained to discriminate between pedestrians and cyclists based on shape and motion characteristics.Head Detection vs. Full-Body Tracking in Dense CrowdsStandard full-body pedestrian detectors (such as Faster R-CNN or standard YOLO configurations) suffer from severe performance degradation in dense crowd environments due to mutual occlusions, which block body features and cause bounding boxes to merge.To resolve this bottleneck, state-of-the-art dense tracking systems employ specialized head detectors, such as the HeadHunter architecture. The human head serves as a highly stable, rigid reference point that is rarely fully occluded even in extremely congested pathways.The superiority of head detection in crowded scenes is quantitatively demonstrated on dense benchmarks. For instance, in a highly crowded scene containing 37 pedestrians, the HeadHunter head detector successfully identified 36 heads, whereas a standard full-body Faster R-CNN model could only resolve 23 pedestrians. Consequently, tracking-by-detection systems deployed in dense environments yield significantly more stable trajectories when configured to detect and associate head coordinates rather than full-body bounding boxes.Temporal Slices and Mathematical Optimization for Line CrossingWhile modern tracking-by-detection pipelines dominate real-time commercial applications, a highly robust, mathematically rigorous paper-based methodology relies on transforming the 3D video volume into 2D temporal slice images. This approach, pioneered in computer vision literature, completely bypasses the need for individual bounding box tracking.The Line Sampling Process and Temporal Slice GenerationThe temporal slice method converts the video sequence into a static 2D image via a continuous line sampling process along the virtual Line of Interest (LOI). Let the virtual LOI in the image plane be defined as a set of pixel coordinates $L = \{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_K\}$. For each frame $t$ in a video sequence of length $T$, the pixel values (intensity or color) along $L$ are extracted. These 1D pixel columns are concatenated chronologically along a temporal axis.The resulting image $I_{\text{slice}}$ is of size $K \times T$, where $K$ represents the spatial length of the line in pixels and $T$ represents the time dimension. In this temporal slice image, moving pedestrians who cross the LOI manifest as distinct, diagonal texture patterns. The angle of these patterns corresponds directly to their crossing velocity, while the width of the pattern represents the physical size of the pedestrian projected onto the line.Count Estimation on Overlapping Sliding WindowsOnce the temporal slice image $I_{\text{slice}}$ is generated, a regression function is trained to estimate the local pedestrian count. The system defines a set of overlapping sliding windows on the temporal slice. Each sliding window $W_i$ corresponds to a specific spatial segment of the LOI and a local time interval $[t_s, t_e]$.For each window, local handcrafted features (such as local binary patterns or HOG) or learned deep features are extracted. A regression model (such as a support vector regressor or a shallow CNN) maps these local features to a continuous value $y_i$, which represents the estimated number of pedestrians crossing that segment of the LOI within that specific temporal window.Integer Programming FormulationBecause the sliding windows overlap, the estimated count $y_i$ in window $W_i$ is mathematically equivalent to the sum of the instantaneous, discrete pedestrian crossings $x_t$ occurring at each frame $t$ within that window's temporal span. To recover the true frame-by-frame crossing events, the problem is formulated as an integer programming (IP) optimization problem.Let $x_t \in \mathbb{Z}^+$ be the unknown, non-negative integer representing the actual number of pedestrians crossing the LOI at frame $t$. Let $H$ be an indicator matrix where $H_{i,t} = 1$ if frame $t$ is contained within the temporal span of sliding window $W_i$, and $0$ otherwise. The objective is to find the integer vector $\mathbf{x} =^\top$ that minimizes the discrepancy between the window sums and the regressed counts $\mathbf{y} = [y_1, y_2, \dots, y_M]^\top$ :$$\min_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left| \sum_{t=1}^{T} H_{i,t} x_t - y_i \right|$$$$\text{s.t. } x_t \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}, \quad \forall t \in \{1, \dots, T\}$$This optimization problem is solved using standard branch-and-bound or cutting-plane algorithms. Once the optimal integer sequence $\mathbf{x}$ is resolved, the cumulative count of pedestrians crossing the line over any specified time interval is obtained simply by integrating the discrete values over that range. This temporal slice formulation is highly robust to variations in pedestrian walking speeds and is immune to the identity-switching errors that plague traditional tracking-by-detection systems in dense crowds.Geometry of Cross-Line Event Detection and Multi-Line HeuristicsIn tracking-by-detection systems, determining whether a pedestrian has crossed the virtual boundary requires evaluating the geometric intersection between their trajectory and the defined Line of Interest (LOI).Single-Line Cross-Line Vector GeometryLet the virtual LOI be defined in the 2D image coordinate space as a directed line segment $L$ bounded by two endpoints $P_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ and $P_2 = (x_2, y_2)$. A tracked pedestrian's movement between consecutive frames $t-1$ and $t$ is represented by a trajectory segment $T$ bounded by coordinates $Q_{t-1} = (x_{t-1}, y_{t-1})$ and $Q_t = (x_t, y_t)$, where $Q$ is typically the centroid or bottom-center of the bounding box.To mathematically evaluate whether segment $T$ intersects segment $L$, the system uses vector cross-products to check the orientation of the point triplets. The orientation function $O(A, B, C)$ is defined by the determinant of the matrix containing their coordinates, which simplifies to :$$O(A, B, C) = (B_y - A_y)(C_x - B_x) - (B_x - A_x)(C_y - B_y)$$The value of $O(A, B, C)$ is positive if the points are oriented in a counter-clockwise direction, negative if clockwise, and zero if they are collinear. A proper intersection between the pedestrian's path $T$ and the virtual line $L$ occurs if and only if the following conditional statement holds true :$$\text{Intersection} = \left( O(P_1, P_2, Q_{t-1}) \cdot O(P_1, P_2, Q_t) < 0 \right) \;\wedge\; \left( O(Q_{t-1}, Q_t, P_1) \cdot O(Q_{t-1}, Q_t, P_2) < 0 \right)$$To determine the direction of the crossing (e.g., entering vs. exiting), the system computes the sign of the vector dot product between the pedestrian's displacement vector $\vec{M} = Q_t - Q_{t-1}$ and the normal vector of the LOI, $\vec{N} = (-(y_2 - y_1), x_2 - x_1)$ :$$\text{Direction} = \text{sign}(\vec{M} \cdot \vec{N})$$If the sign is positive, the trajectory crosses from top-to-bottom (or left-to-right, depending on line orientation); a negative sign indicates the opposite direction. This triggers the respective directional counters to increment.Multi-Line and Four-Line Crossing HeuristicsIn high-density, mixed pedestrian environments such as urban street intersections, conventional single-line crossing methods suffer from high failure rates. Diagonal trajectories, erratic paths, and brief occlusions often cause a pedestrian's tracked centroid to skip over the virtual line without triggering the intersection conditional.To resolve these errors, researchers developed the Maximum Four-Lines Counting Method. Instead of drawing a singular line of interest, four parallel virtual lines are defined near the crosswalk region. The geometric intersection algorithm evaluates the pedestrian's trajectory against all four lines simultaneously. If a pedestrian crosses any of the lines, a counter associated with that specific line is incremented.Rather than summing the counts—which would result in massive overcounting—the system evaluates the complete trajectory over time and takes the maximum count from the four lines as the final result. This ensures that any legitimate crossing is recorded even if occlusions disrupt tracking along three of the lines, significantly reducing missed detections.Furthermore, an additional directional guidance line is defined parallel to the pedestrian flow direction. To count flows under these extreme conditions, systems shift toward joint density map and velocity-field regression.Mathematical Map DecompositionThe core formulation of the two-phase approach treats the cross-line crowd counting problem as the joint estimation of two pixel-level maps from pairs of video frames: a crowd density map $D_t(p)$ and a crowd velocity map $V_t(p)$ at pixel location $p = (x, y)$. Under the assumption that the local density and flow velocity remain constant over the tiny temporal interval between consecutive frames $t$ and $t+1$, the instantaneous crossing-line count map $C_t(p)$ is defined as the element-wise multiplication (Hadamard product) of the density and velocity maps :$$C_t(p) = D_t(p) \otimes V_t(p)$$The ground truth density map $D_t(p)$ is generated from dot head annotations $P_t = \{P_t^1, \dots, P_t^n\}$ using kernel density estimation with an adaptive Gaussian kernel, where the kernel size $\sigma_P$ is proportional to the distance to nearest neighbors to compensate for perspective distortions :$$D_t(p) = \sum_{P \in P_t} \mathcal{N}(p; P, \sigma_P^2)$$The ground truth velocity map $V_t(p)$ is a vector field representing the pixel-level velocity of pedestrians along the $x$- and $y$-directions, extracted from optical flow estimation.Two-Phase Optimization SchemeThe deep convolutional neural network is optimized using a structured two-phase scheme to solve the joint regression problem :Phase I (Decomposed Pre-training): The network uses a shared bottom convolutional network to extract high-level spatio-temporal features from consecutive frames. The architecture then splits into two parallel, deconvolutional decoder heads. One head is trained to regress the crowd density map $D_t$, while the other head regresses the crowd velocity map $V_t$. This provides a highly descriptive spatial-temporal initialization for the network.Phase II (End-to-End Fine-Tuning): The estimated density and velocity maps are combined via an element-wise multiplication layer to generate the final predicted crossing-line count map $\tilde{C}_t(p)$. The entire network is fine-tuned end-to-end using a pixel-level $L_2$ loss function against the ground-truth crowd counting map $C_t(p)$ :$$L_{\text{second}} = \sum_{p} \left\| \tilde{C}_t(p) - C_t(p) \right\|^2$$Spatio-Temporal LOI IntegrationTo extract the directional counts $c_{1,t}$ and $c_{2,t}$ crossing the virtual LOI at frame $t$, the predicted crossing-line count map vector $\tilde{C}_t(p) = (\tilde{C}_{t,x}(p), \tilde{C}_{t,y}(p))$ is projected onto the normal vector of the LOI segment. Integrating the projected values along the spatial coordinates of the LOI yields the instantaneous directional counts :$$c_{1,t} = \sum_{\{p \in \text{LOI} \mid \cos(\theta_p) \ge 0\}} \sqrt{\tilde{C}_{t,x}(p)^2 + \tilde{C}_{t,y}(p)^2} \cdot \cos(\theta_p)$$$$c_{2,t} = \sum_{\{p \in \text{LOI} \mid \cos(\theta_p) < 0\}} \sqrt{\tilde{C}_{t,x}(p)^2 + \tilde{C}_{t,y}(p)^2} \cdot (-\cos(\theta_p))$$where $\theta_p$ represents the angle between the LOI's normal vector and the flow vector at pixel location $p$. Summing these instantaneous values over a temporal window $T$ yields the final cumulative crossing counts, offering an occlusion-invariant flow rate estimation without discrete object tracking.Video Individual Counting: Weak Supervision, Grouping Priors, and AttentionWhile cross-line methods capture flow rates, they fail to quantify unique visual presence across unconstrained, wide-area surveillance scenes. The modern frontier of crowd analytics has shifted toward Video Individual Counting (VIC), which aims to count every unique individual appearing in a video sequence exactly once. Mathematically, the total individual count $N_{\text{total}}$ in a video of length $T$ sampled at interval $\sigma$ is formulated as :$$N_{\text{total}} = N_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{T/\sigma} N_{\text{in}}^{k\sigma}$$where $N_0$ is the count of pedestrians in the initial frame and $N_{\text{in}}^{k\sigma}$ represents the number of new individuals entering the field of view (inflows) in subsequent frames.Supervised VIC: DRNetThe pioneer of this task, the Decomposition and Reasoning Network (DRNet), solves the correspondence problem using a localization-then-association paradigm. DRNet first localizes pedestrian heads in the initial frame to determine $N_0$. In subsequent frames, it reasons about the cross-frame co-existence of individuals using a differentiable optimal transport formulation, thereby directly calculating the newly entering pedestrian inflows ($N_{\text{in}}$) and exits (outflows). However, DRNet is computationally expensive and relies on dense trajectory annotations, which are highly labor-intensive to produce.Weakly Supervised VIC (WVIC) and CGNetTo reduce annotation bottlenecks, researchers formulated Weakly Supervised Video Individual Counting (WVIC). WVIC completely eliminates the need for individual trajectory tracking labels. Instead, it uses identity-agnostic group-level annotations indicating only whether a localized pedestrian represents an inflow or outflow in the current frame.The benchmark framework for WVIC is CGNet (Contrastive learning with Group-level matching), which operates through three highly integrated modules :Image-Level Locator: A coordinate localization network (e.g., FIDT) that detects pedestrian coordinates and crops localized patches.Encoder: A deep feature extractor (e.g., ConvNeXt) that processes the cropped patches, outputting highly discriminative normalized 1D representation vectors $f_u^i$ for each individual.Memory-Based Individual Count Predictor (MCP): A non-trainable inference module that caches historical templates of individuals in memory to prevent ID switches during long-term occlusions.Group-Level Matching and Soft Contrastive LossInstead of matching individual trajectories, CGNet aggregates individuals in adjacent frames $V_i$ and $V_j$ into group-level sets based on weak inflow ($I^j$) and outflow ($O^i$) labels :Shared Individuals ($X$ and $Y$): Pedestrians present in both frames ($X = \{p_u^i \mid O_u^i = 0\}$ and $Y = \{p_u^j \mid I_u^j = 0\}$).Outflow Individuals ($\hat{X}$): Pedestrians present only in the previous frame ($P_i - X$).Inflow Individuals ($\hat{Y}$): Pedestrians present only in the current frame ($P_j - Y$).An all-to-all similarity matrix $S$ is calculated between features of frame $V_i$ and $V_j$ :$$S = \begin{bmatrix} (F_X^i)^\top F_Y^j & (F_X^i)^\top F_{\hat{Y}}^j \\ (F_{\hat{X}}^i)^\top F_Y^j & (F_{\hat{X}}^i)^\top F_{\hat{Y}}^j \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_0 & S_1 \\ S_2 & S_3 \end{bmatrix}$$To optimize the encoder without discrete identity labels, CGNet enforces a Soft Contrastive Loss ($L_{\text{scon}}$) over the shared sub-matrix $S_0$. This loss formulation treats similarity as an Optimal Transport problem solved via the Sinkhorn algorithm. Let $\Omega$ be the soft matching assignment matrix and $C$ be the contrastive similarity matrix calculated via softmax over similarities :$$L_{\text{scon}} = \min_{\Omega} - \sum_{u=1}^{m} \sum_{v=1}^{m} \Omega_{u,v} C_{u,v}$$This optimizes $S_0$ to behave as a permutation matrix, while pushing mismatch regions $S_1$ and $S_2$ to zero. To prevent feature collapse of the unique single-frame elements in $S_3$, a Hinge $L_1$ loss ($L_{\text{hinge}}$) is applied to maintain representation diversity :$$L_{\text{hinge}} = \frac{\sum \max(0, S_3 - \theta)}{(n_i - m)(n_j - m)}$$Shared Density Map-Guided Network (SDNet)While CGNet relies on a "localization-then-association" pipeline, localization frequently fails on small, heavily occluded targets. To address this, the Shared Density map-guided Network (SDNet) completely bypasses localized coordinate extraction.SDNet introduces a Depth-wise Cross-Frame Attention (DCFA) module. DCFA takes multi-scale feature maps from consecutive frames as inputs, computing cross-frame attention to directly decode a shared density map ($D_{\text{shared}}$) representing only the co-existent pedestrians. The individual inflow ($D_{\text{in}}$) and outflow ($D_{\text{out}}$) density maps are subsequently derived by subtracting the shared density map from the global density maps ($D_{\text{global}}$) of each frame :$$D_{\text{in}} = D_{\text{global}, j} - D_{\text{shared}}$$The final unique pedestrian count is computed simply by summing the integrated inflow density maps across all temporal frames, bypassing any explicit association step.OMAN: One-to-Many MatchingThe OMAN (One-to-Many mAtchiNg) model introduces a vital paradigm shift. Recognizing that traditional VIC and MOT methods suffer from rigid one-to-one (O2O) association constraints—which collapse during occlusions—OMAN relaxes matching to a one-to-many (O2M) formulation.OMAN is built upon two highly intuitive physical priors:Social Grouping Prior: Pedestrians naturally move in groups, sharing visual context and spatial-temporal trajectories. When an individual is occluded, matching them to their surrounding "group" prevents track loss. OMAN integrates an Implicit Context Generator (ICG) to embed this group context into individual descriptors.Spatial-Temporal Displacement Prior: Pedestrians cannot teleport; their movement between successive frames is physically bounded. This bound restricts the search space for associations locally, preventing erroneous distant matching.By coupling these priors with a One-to-Many Pairwise Matcher (OMPM), OMAN achieves state-of-the-art results on challenging benchmarks such as WuhanMetroCrowd, CroHD, and SenseCrowd, demonstrating massive reductions in identity switches. Another framework, GD3A (Global Density map Decomposition via group-wise Descriptor Association), similarly leverages optimal transport with an adaptive dustbin score to perform global video individual counting and tracking on moving drone platforms, demonstrating strong robustness in large-scale scenes.Evaluation Metrics and Benchmark AnalysisTo rigorously evaluate the performance of pedestrian tracking and counting systems, researchers employ a diverse set of standardized metrics that isolate detection, association, and localization errors.Standard Tracking MetricsCLEAR MOT (MOTA & MOTP): The Multi-Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA) score is a cumulative metric summarizing three error types: false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and identity switches (IDSW). Multi-Object Tracking Precision (MOTP) measures the average spatial overlap (IoU) between true positive tracks and ground truth bounding boxes.IDF1: Measures the ratio of correctly identified detections over the average number of ground-truth and predicted detections, focusing specifically on how well a tracker preserves a unique identity over long temporal spans.HOTA (Higher Order Tracking Accuracy): Formulated to address the imbalances of MOTA and IDF1, HOTA explicitly balances detection accuracy (DetA) and association accuracy (AssA) into a single unified score. It decomposes into sub-metrics that evaluate five distinct error types separately, aligning closely with human visual evaluations of tracking performance.IDEucl: The Identity Euclidean distance metric evaluates a tracker's ability to preserve unique identities over the longest possible spatial stretch in image coordinate space, establishing a direct correspondence between visual tracking performance and physical crowd trajectories.Frequency of Relative Position Switch: Measures the number of times an object's relative 2D position switches with respect to its neighbors between adjacent frames, quantifying tracking stability in dense, crossing crowd flows.Pedestrian Tracking and VIC BenchmarksMOT17 & MOT20: Standard multi-object tracking benchmarks. Utilizing an optimized, lightweight detector (e.g., YOLOv8n or YOLO11) exported to ONNX format and executed on a CPU-only environment via ONNX Runtime offers an extremely cost-effective, real-time solution (processing at $>30$ FPS). This pipeline must be integrated with geometric stabilization algorithms—specifically bottom boundary suppression, a 15-frame wandering suppression filter, and a gate-based auxiliary buffer zone—to eliminate double counts and track drops.For dense, heavily congested public spaces, such as subway platforms or railway ticket barriers, individual tracking models inevitably fail due to continuous occlusions. In these congested scenarios, the system should deploy the Two-Phase Deep Neural Network architecture. By jointly regressing pixel-level density and velocity fields to directly compute cross-line counts, this model bypasses discrete tracking entirely, delivering highly accurate flow statistics in crowded environments.For unconstrained wide-area surveillance, public parks, or dynamic platforms like moving drones, static line-crossing approaches are structurally inadequate. The system should deploy a weakly supervised Video Individual Counting (VIC) framework, such as CGNet or the attention-guided SDNet. By utilizing group-level matching losses and depth-wise cross-frame attention, these architectures count unique individuals across dynamic, changing viewpoints, providing robust analytics for smart city planning and crowd safety management.
